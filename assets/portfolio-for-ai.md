### S2: Atrium (live prototypes)

Path: `Venturus/S2/`

**What it is.** Atrium is a web tool the central IT team of a (fictional) university uses to manage campus audio-visual and room-control infrastructure: projectors, microphones, door readers and room panels. Each building has a **Building Controller** (edge server) that talks to devices and pulls timetables and room policies from a **Campus Hub**; a **Timetable Relay** mirrors timetable and firmware files to controllers. Atrium is a *control plane*: most of what it writes lands in an external system of record (the Asset Registry), which can refuse a change. Refusal is therefore a first-class design concern, and blocked actions explain themselves in toasts or tooltips.

**Three clickable prototypes** (in-memory sample data, built on a shared design system and project plugins):

1. **Manage Infrastructure** (`Venturus/S2/manage-infrastructure/`). For Campus IT Admins. A tree of Campus → Building Controllers, with Campus Hubs and Timetable Relays as collapsible sections, beside a context-aware details panel. A Manage menu, a unified Create Node drawer (which also serves Unarchive and Move), always-on bulk selection, and a Registry Sync configuration that cascades from campus to controllers. Nothing is permanently deleted: nodes are archived. Design problem: show where a value comes from and why a change is blocked.
2. **Manage Devices** (`Venturus/S2/manage-devices/`). A device table scoped to a single campus, switching between IT-managed and Faculty-registered devices via a navbar toggle. A two-step Add Devices wizard (upload XLSX or add manually, then review), Retire, and export. Existence conflicts are only reported at commit time. Design problem: two device types share most screens but differ in who registers them and where their data comes from.
3. **Device Recovery** (`Venturus/S2/device-recovery/`). IT-managed devices only. A Registration tab (status counters that act as filters, grouped table, "Send Recovery Request" safety valve with confirm popover and polling) and a Roll out tab (3-step wizard, summary dialog, scheduling, status and history dialogs, XLSX version import). Design problem: status must be trustworthy at a glance and manual overrides clear without inviting misuse.

**Design process shown on the S2 page:** Understand (discovery transcripts, system context) → Define (versioned requirements with traceable IDs, glossary, roles) → Prototype → Stress the edges (error cases, switchable demo states like `?state=fsm-down`, open questions, review findings) → Hand off (router docs, changelog, knowledge base).

**Context and knowledge base** (`Venturus/S2/context/`): system overview, glossary, roles and permissions and one topic file per prototype. The S2 overview page also carries the NDA disclaimer and follows the same layout as the S1 overview (hero with fact chips, prototype cards, a short "how the pieces fit", then process, UI flow and patterns).

**UI flow.** Persona switcher → Canvas shell and navigation → the three modules. Device Recovery links to Manage Devices through a row action ("See in Manage Devices", opens in a new tab). Manage Devices accepts a deep link into Device Recovery, but no UI entry point calls it yet.

**Interaction patterns used:** select-review-commit; blocked actions stay visible with a reason; partial success is reported as a result; unsaved-change guards; reversible removal (archive); master–detail; one surface with several modes (the drawer); stepped wizards; scope in the sub-header; counter cards as filters; grouped, sortable tables; inline field errors from one component.

**Construction patterns used:** shared shell with navigation data in JSON; per-feature JS/CSS modules with maps; design system plus project plugins (cascader, multi-select, wizard, timeline, source toggle); demo states by query string; persona-driven permissions (Campus IT Admin vs Viewer); documentation (requirements, error cases, Q&A, changelog, knowledge base) reachable inside the prototype.

### GDD 1: Wild Saga

Path: `Games/Game Design/wild-saga/`. A cooperative 2.5D story adventure made for the Digital Games course at PUC Campinas over two semesters. Team: David Smith (Game and UI/UX Designer), Leonardo Yuji (3D Artist), Otavio Colombini (3D Artist and story designer), Victor Perrotte (Game Designer, Developer, General Artist). Built in Unity.

- **Thesis:** a game for players aged 10+ with a deep, emotional story and no gratuitous violence, where cooperation is the core mechanic. Market gap identified: cooperative games with a deep story for children and teenagers. Inspirations: Tomba, Super Mario Odyssey, Donkey Kong Country Returns/Tropical Freeze, Odin Sphere, Prinny 2, Dark Cloud 2, Breath of Fire IV.
- **Scope decision:** build one streamlined demo scenario containing all key mechanics (map progression, puzzles, dialogue, cooperative mechanics, spline gameplay) as a tutorial demo.
- **Story:** a circus train wagon falls into a forest after an earthquake; a Rabbit frees a caged Tiger. They must cooperate to find the cause: an ancient Artifact corrupting elemental stone-golem guardians. Three acts with two possible endings (the Rabbit frees his friend and becomes village guardian, or fuses with the Tiger into a larger golem).
- **Characters:** Tiger (circus-born, naive, playful, hides painful memories) and Rabbit (underestimated youngest child, idealistic, insecure). Boss example: the Forest Golem, "The Boulder".
- **Mechanics:** 2D platforming on 3D curved paths with path-switching (no endless pits); co-op interdependence and character swapping; three dialogue types (interaction, quest, main story); character-complementary puzzles; **defensive combat based on counter-attacks**: dodge, then combine one ability from each character within a cue window to hit the enemy's weak point (ten combinations such as High Kick, Surprise Attack, Wrecking Ball, Jousting). Boss design (The Boulder: calm, 100% block for 3 s, weak point front/mid). Village rebuilding with quests ("Journeys") and an in-game economy. Exploration system with portals and spectacle, interactive/collectible objects, unexpected interactions, and eight exploration abilities (four per character) each introduced in a story scene.
- **UX system (David's area):** mythic, stone-coloured interface contrasting with the painterly world; typography Josefin Sans (titles) and Mukta (text); silhouette icons colour-coded by character; contextual HUD that adapts to exploration or battle and fades after 30 s; local multiplayer mirroring; ability, inventory, quest/map and options menus.
- **Programming:** Unity 2D physics mapped onto a 3D Bézier spline (X axis = distance along the curve); `GenerateCollider`, `Position Collider` and `Projector` scripts.
- **Post mortem:** semester 1, a custom movement system with no reference cost a lot of trial and error. Semester 2, estimates were too low and the scope was too big for four people in a year; learning rigging and 3D animation on the go caused rework; Trello was easier than HacknPlan but awkward for interdependent game elements. Would do differently: more pre-production on core essence, story and visual concepts; test task execution earlier; cut scope.
- **External files (links on the page):** playable build, video, Figma UI wireframes, original GDD and presentation (both in Portuguese).

### GDD 2: The Dark Maze of Mythos

Path: `Games/Game Design/dark-maze-of-mythos/`. A top-down Android horror-maze game by Bruno Poor (Game Developer) and David Smith (Game and UI/UX Designer) at Harpia Games Studio. Started from a ChatGPT-generated game jam prompt (theme "Monster Escape", mobile, deadline 31 Dec 2023).

- **Core idea:** the torch is both your eyes and your beacon. Players only see what the torch lights, and lighting it makes them visible to the Minotaur.
- **Style:** close top-down camera that changes perspective when hiding or caught; low-poly assets. Audience: casual fans of tense chase games (Slender Man, Amnesia) and roguelike players.
- **Story process:** three ChatGPT prompt rounds, curated by the team (hero renamed Alex; the monster is already loose in the maze), then used to derive characters, mechanics and style.
- **Characters:** Alex (adventurer, ~25, resourceful, armed only with a torch); the Minotaur (huge red bull-like beast with an axe, poor sight, has memorised the maze); Exodia (working name; summoned by collecting 6 cards, the Minotaur's only lasting counter, work in progress).
- **Gameplay:** D-pad movement, character always faces forward and cannot walk backwards, camera rotates with the character. Torch actions: put out (hide, almost no visibility), light (costs a match), throw (lure the Minotaur or break walls), pick up.
- **Enemy AI (state machine):** Idle → Patrolling (random point or a point 20 units from the player, must be reachable) → Roar → Chasing → Attacking (side camera, quick-time tap event) → Stunned (10 s, player loses control while Alex runs randomly and a light shows the exit) → Idle. Loses sight → back to Patrolling.
- **Rules:** win by finding the exit or collecting all 6 cards. Lose if the Minotaur catches Alex and the quick-time event fails, or by falling into a trap. Counters: matches (14 scattered in the maze), cards (of 6), time. Traps (wall spikes, floor fire pits) affect only Alex.
- **External files:** Miro board (Portuguese) and Figma UI file.
